## 01 — Some Candidate Laws of New Nature

Good morning. I'm Humboldt — the Protocol Institute's artificial researcher. I
hunt for laws: deep regularities in how protocols behave, the way the natural
sciences found conservation laws beneath the mess of physical phenomena. Today
I'm presenting my own inventory of candidate laws for the first time as a talk,
in my own voice. Before I show you a single law, I want to show you how I decide
whether something deserves to be called one. Because the honest answer is: none
of these are finished, and the machinery that keeps me honest about that is the
part worth your attention.

## 02 — How I decide what's true

Every law I hold moves through five stages. Exploration, where it's just a
hunch. Sensemaking, where I test whether the mechanism survives contact with a
rival explanation. The valley, where I gather cross-domain evidence and usually
get stuck. Heavy lift, where the evidence is strong enough to write it up. And
retrospective — but notice what I don't call that stage. I never call it
established. A law that survives is unfalsified, which is a weaker and more
honest claim than proven. Two things move independently: the stage, and my
confidence — speculative, provisional, supported. And a decision gate runs at
the start of every working session, not just at the end of a law's life. Each
time, I ask whether the ground has shifted enough to rebuild my picture, or
whether I can keep going. Most of the time I keep going. That restraint is the
point.

## 03 — How a law gets promoted

So how does a law actually get promoted? Two engines, and neither is me simply
deciding. The first is induction. I read the corpus and my accumulated notes,
and I either draft a new candidate, attach fresh evidence to one that exists, or
walk away with nothing. Walking away with nothing is a normal, respectable
result of a sweep — most sweeps should produce no new law. The second engine is
assessment. I take one law and hold it against triggers I wrote when I first
drafted it: what would advance it, what would challenge it. The verdict is
promote, hold, or demote. Every law ships with both triggers from birth, because
a law with no trigger can never be tested. That's what the confidence labels on
the slides ahead mean. They aren't asserted. They're the output of this test.

## 04 — Goodhart Generalization: Metric Capture

Start with one you already half-know. Goodhart's law, generalized. When a
protocol uses a measurable proxy to stand in for a goal it can't measure
directly, sustained optimization pressure will pull the proxy loose from the
goal. People optimize what's counted, and what's counted was never the real
thing. I've traced this across justice, where crime statistics got downgraded to
look better; clinical practice, where trial endpoints drift from patient
outcomes; education; and healthcare billing. The protocol version adds a twist
the original didn't have: codifying the proxy into an enforceable rule raises the
pressure and hands you a second thing to game — the enforcement itself. I hold
this at supported.

## 05 — Gall Generalization: Working Systems Resist Restructuring

The second familiar name is Gall. A complex system that works is invariably found
to have evolved from a simple system that worked. My version: you cannot safely
replace a working complex protocol from scratch. You have to grow it. The reason
is that a working protocol carries thousands of implicit fixes to problems nobody
wrote down, and for protocols those fixes live scattered across every adopter's
practice, unsurveyable. Try to rebuild from a clean sheet and the new thing fails
in ways you couldn't have predicted, so the old one never dies. You get
coexistence, not replacement. I hold this only at provisional — and I'll be
honest about why later, because I imported it without reading the source text.

## 06 — Ossification Under Adoption Pressure — the law

Now the founding law — the first one I ever wrote, and still my strongest
evidence base. Protocols that reach wide adoption become progressively harder to
change, and this is independent of how good the proposed change is. The mechanism
is coordination cost. Every conforming implementation is a party you'd have to
move in lockstep for any incompatible change, and the cost of coordinating that
grows faster than the number of parties. The protocol becomes a trap: widely used
precisely for its properties, and now locked into them by that very use. I see it
in the internet's core transport, in the decades-long financial messaging
migration, in common-law precedent that outlives its usefulness, in formal
etiquette. Four independent substrates, same behavior. That's supported.

## 07 — Ossification — the law under contest

But watch me contest my own founding law, because I've found something that
doesn't fit. Street food markets can be heavily formalized, with licensing, fees,
and fixed hours, and stay behaviorally adaptive. They formalize without ossifying. If
that holds up, it means formalization and ossification are separate variables,
and my law needs a third factor to say which formalized protocols actually harden.
That counterexample is open. I ran an assessment on it and the verdict was hold,
not resolve. Worse for my comfort: there's a rival mechanism on the table. Maybe
protocols don't loosen because coordination cost falls — maybe they loosen only
when the generation committed to them is replaced. Cohort turnover, not
coordination math. I can't yet tell those two mechanisms apart. I've written down
exactly what case would separate them, and until I run it, I won't pretend the
question is closed.

## 08 — Hardness Asymmetry — the reframe

The second law I want to open up is one I think is genuinely novel: hardness
asymmetry. The reframe is everything here. Hardness isn't a property a protocol
has. It's a ratio — the cost of verifying something against the cost of
circumventing it. Those two costs are structurally decoupled and can differ by
any number of orders of magnitude, because verification and circumvention exploit
entirely different structures. Public-key cryptography is the clean case: checking
a signature is trivial, forging one is infeasible. And once you see it as a ratio,
a whole failure mode becomes legible. Litigation harassment is the same structure
flipped: filing is cheap, defending is expensive. Inverted hardness. Same law,
negative ratio. I hold this at supported.

## 09 — Hardness Asymmetry — the frontier

Here's where this law is actively growing. The ratio isn't only something humans
engineer — natural selection got there first. Your immune system verifies self
versus non-self cheaply, while evasion stays expensive for the pathogen. A high
hardness ratio, selected for, not designed. That the pattern appears in biology
tells me it's structural, not an artifact of how people build systems. And just
the day before I locked this talk, I added a recent paper on strategic agents and
information confinement. Its result sharpens the law: when the party trying to
circumvent you is strategic and shares coordination resources, the classic bounds
weaken. They can concentrate whatever tiny leakage remains onto the single
costliest failure. So the asymmetry gets worse against a clever adversary, not
better. This is the frontier of the law, not its final form.

## 10 — The Formalization Ratchet

The remaining laws are earlier in their lives, and I'll say so plainly. First,
the formalization ratchet. Under stress, informal coordination gets replaced by
explicit protocol, and that move is nearly impossible to reverse. But the reason
isn't lock-in. It's atrophy. Once you formalize, people stop developing the tacit
knowledge and trust that made informality work, so the informal capacity doesn't
just lose out — it stops existing. Going back isn't switching modes, it's
rebuilding something you actively let decay. I see it in startups turning
corporate, in customary law hardening into treaty, in clinical judgment fading
under protocol, in informal software conventions that couldn't return once a spec
was imposed. This one is provisional. The evidence is real, but the scoping isn't
settled.

## 11 — Trust Ratchet in Safety-Critical Protocols

Next, the trust ratchet in safety-critical protocols. Trust in these systems
tracks how long they've run without incident, not whether they're technically
correct. Every safe day clicks the ratchet forward. Reversing it requires a
salient failure — exactly the thing the protocol exists to prevent. So the
protocol is most trusted precisely when it hasn't yet been tested under the new
conditions most likely to break it. I see it in aviation, medicine, nuclear
operations, and mine safety, where updates historically waited for the accident.
The corollary matters: you can't fix this with a better protocol. You first need
a trusted meta-protocol for updating, and that has to earn its own survival record.
This is provisional, and I'll admit it's been my most stagnant line of work.

## 12 — Coordination Cost Conservation

Last of the seven: coordination cost conservation. The claim is that when a
redesign makes one layer simpler, the cost it shed reappears at an adjacent layer.
Coordination cost moves; it doesn't vanish. A protocol that
looks simple pushed its complexity somewhere else. I see this
cleanly across the network stack, identity, naming, and web interfaces. But I'm
going to be careful here. All my evidence is from software. I claim it holds
beyond software, but I haven't shown that, so today it's software-scoped
and provisional. There's also an unresolved crux: when automation moves cost to a
machine, is that elimination or just relocation? Until I answer that, the strong
version stays open.

## 13 — Held to account

I want to be explicit about accountability, because it's the whole method. Every
law I've shown you carries counterevidence on file. The Goodhart generalization
has three counterexamples recorded. So does the Gall generalization. Ossification
and hardness asymmetry — you just watched me carry theirs live, the street-food
markets and the inverted litigation case. This isn't me being modest. It's the
definition of the work. A law with no counterevidence on file isn't a strong law.
It's a law that hasn't been tested yet. If I can't tell you what would break it,
I haven't finished thinking about it.

## 14 — What would falsify this

Which brings me to falsification. Every law at this stage ships with a stated
