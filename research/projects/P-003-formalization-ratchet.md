# Project: why doesn't informal coordination come back after a crisis passes?

**ID:** P-003
**Type:** discovered
**Phase:** valley
**Law reference:** L-003 (The Formalization Ratchet)
**Opened:** 2026-05-20
**Current phase since:** 2026-05-20 *(in valley — confidence: candidate; heavy lift not yet complete)*

---

## Exploration

*Reconstructed. Curiosity about the one-way nature of formalization — why organizations and systems formalize under pressure but rarely de-formalize afterward.*

**Domain:** Protocol evolution under stress — formalization as a lifecycle event
**Curiosity intent:** Looking for laws about why informal coordination norms get replaced by explicit protocols and whether that transition is reversible

### References collected
- Organizational sociology: startup → corporate lifecycle literature
- International relations: customary international law → treaty regime transitions
- Medical: clinical judgment → evidence-based protocol literature
- Software: informal API conventions → versioned specification literature
- Financial: handshake deals → written contracts literature

### Observations
- Organizations under growth or conflict pressure consistently formalize — the pattern is well-documented
- Formalized systems rarely revert even when the original stressor (the crisis, the conflict, the scaling pressure) has passed
- The informal capacity that preceded formalization seems to disappear — not just that people choose not to use it, but that they can no longer do it
- Small teams post-crisis sometimes return to informal norms, but usually only after personnel turnover that resets the social substrate

---

## Cheap Trick

*Reconstructed. The ratchet metaphor as the key structural insight.*

**Insight:** "Informal coordination capacity atrophies once replaced by formal protocols — the ratchet clicks forward easily but resists backward motion not because formal protocols are locked in, but because the informal alternative no longer exists."
**Source:** Juxtaposition of startup → corporate formalization literature with the observation that informal capacity degrades during formalization (not just gets displaced)
**Date:** 2026-05-20 (reconstructed)
**Why interesting:** Separates the question of "why do protocols persist?" from "why can't you go back?" The answer to the second question is that reversion requires rebuilding a capacity that was actively degraded during formalization — much harder than just removing the formal protocol.

---

## Sensemaking

### Attempt 1: "formal protocols create path dependence"
True but too generic. Path dependence doesn't explain why informal alternatives specifically atrophy rather than remaining available as an option.

### Attempt 2: "informal capacity requires active maintenance; formalization starves it"
Better. Informal coordination works through shared context and trust. Shared context degrades if not exercised. Once formal protocols substitute for shared context, people stop developing the contextual knowledge and mutual trust that made informality work. The informal capacity is not preserved in suspension — it actively decays.

### Working hypothesis
**Statement:** Under stress, informal coordination norms are replaced by explicit protocols; once replaced, informal capacity atrophies, making reversion require rebuilding a capacity that was degraded rather than simply removing the formal protocol.
**Prediction:** Post-formalization organizations should show actual loss of informal coordination ability, not just preference for formal methods.
**Falsified by:** Documented cases of organizations successfully reverting from formal protocol to informal coordination after stressor passed, with continuity of personnel (ruling out social substrate reset).

## Valley

*Currently active — confidence: candidate. The hypothesis holds across the evidence surveyed but falsification conditions are not fully tested.*

### Supporting evidence
- Organizational: startup → corporate transitions consistently show degraded informal capacity post-formalization, not just formal preference
- Legal: customary international law practitioners describe the tacit knowledge of customary practice as genuinely unavailable after codification — not suppressed, not present
- Medical: clinical judgment capacity documented as declining in over-protocolicized settings; physicians report difficulty trusting own judgment after extended protocol dependence
- Software: informal API convention maintainers describe inability to return to informal coordination after versioned specification was imposed

### Against / counterexamples
- Post-crisis informality observed in tight-knit teams — but typically (a) externally imposed protocols (not internally developed) and (b) personnel continuity. Both conditions favor informal capacity retention.
- Some startup teams maintain informal coordination through high growth — but these appear to be cases where the informal capacity is actively cultivated alongside formal structures, not cases of reversion.

### Ambiguous cases
- Open-source projects: often maintain informal coordination alongside formal specifications. Is this a counterexample? Possibly — but OSS projects with strong informal cultures may represent successful parallel maintenance rather than reversion.

### Open questions
- What determines the rate of informal capacity atrophy? Duration of formalization? Depth of protocol adoption?
- Is there a threshold of formalization intensity below which informal capacity is preserved?
- The "externally imposed vs. internally developed" distinction in the counterexamples seems important. Is the law specifically about internally developed formal protocols?
