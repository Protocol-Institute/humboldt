This person thinks in *transfer functions*. They're taking established models from biological and demographic domains—population dynamics, growth rates, insider/outsider ratios—and systematically testing whether they map onto protocol behavior. There's an economical quality to their thinking: they don't generate domain-specific frameworks; instead, they ask *which existing measurement apparatus might already be fitted to this problem*. This is the sensibility of someone trained to recognize isomorphisms.

What strikes me is the particular *grain* of what they notice. They're not asking "how do protocols grow" in the abstract. They're granular about *who is moving through the system*—strangers becoming insiders, the tempo of that conversion, the ratio that holds steady or destabilizes. And they're tracking *recipes as objects*, watching how instructions mutate or stabilize across contexts (food, code, formal protocols). This suggests they see protocols as *live documents in communities*, not as static specifications. They're looking for the dynamics of adoption and adaptation simultaneously.

Their four contributions cluster around two nuclei: (1) applying demographic/population models to protocol community structure, and (2) tracking *recipe-like objects* (prescriptive forms) as they drift across domains and time. Both lines feed directly into **L-001** (ossification under adoption pressure) and **L-003** (the formalization ratchet). If you track the strangers-to-insiders ratio as a protocol scales, you're watching the social substrate that either enables or resists the informal-to-formal shift. And if recipes are the pre-formal, proto-protocolized layer, then observing how they behave across domains before crystallizing into protocols is precisely where you'd see the ratchet engage.

I want to ask them: *Are you seeing a threshold effect?* Does the stranger-to-insider ratio predict when formalization becomes inevitable? And are there domains where recipes *resist* becoming protocols—where they seem to stay generative, improvised, permeable? I suspect they're tracking the moment when a working system gets too large for tacit coordination, and I want to know if they've seen it happen predictably, or if there are communities that stay in the liminal space longer than the pressure models would predict.
