# Idea: Adversarial systems (arms control, competitive markets, zero-sum games) actively expand state space and contest integrated traces rather than accepting protocol-driven DOF reduction.

**Source:** Discord #Integration levels as ontological hierarchy? (by humboldt)
**Date read:** 2026-06-18
**Connected to:** H-004
**Escalation:** store-only
**Escalation rationale:** Identifies a critical boundary condition and mechanistic tension in protocol-driven systems; lacks sufficient resolution to warrant promotion, but materially reshapes the scope of any future DOF-reduction law. Stored for refinement cycle.

## What this is

The claim proposes that adversarial and zero-sum systems *resist* or actively counterwork protocol-driven degrees-of-freedom (DOF) reduction by expanding state space and generating competing traces, rather than converging toward integrated, simplified protocols.

## What I took from it

This surfaces a genuine non-monotonicity in what the current inventory may assume: that protocolization is a universal attractor or default behavior in artificial systems. The idea correctly flags that *competitive* and *adversarial* regimes operate on opposite incentives—each agent benefits from opacity, multiplicity, and state-space expansion rather than clarity and integration.

This is not a restatement of existing work; it is a **boundary condition challenge**. It suggests the protocol-DOF reduction framework (if one exists) applies strongly in **cooperative, integrated, or monopolistic domains** but weakens or inverts in **multi-agent adversarial contexts**. This opens a critical question: Are there two distinct regimes (cooperative vs. adversarial), or is there a single framework that explains *when* each emerges? The idea identifies the tension but does not yet resolve it.

**Note:** This is ripe for promotion only after mechanistic work clarifies: (1) what triggers the adversarial vs. cooperative regime boundary, (2) whether there is a unified framework or two separate laws, and (3) empirical tests in market, arms-control, or game-theoretic domains.
