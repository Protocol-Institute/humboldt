## What this is

A policy/governance paper arguing that current legal and contractual frameworks (ToS, informal practices, bot-blocking) fail to distinguish between malicious automation and legitimately-authorized AI agents, creating barriers to beneficial agent deployment on the agentic web. The work is normative—proposing that infrastructure redesign is needed—rather than presenting empirical mechanisms or theoretical laws.

## What I took from it

This is fundamentally a governance gap analysis: it identifies that existing institutional rules (ToS, platform blocking policies, legal statutes) were written for a pre-agentic internet and now create friction. The core claim—that platforms block agents indiscriminately—is a sociological observation about current practice, not a mechanism governing agent behavior itself or the dynamics of protocolized systems.

The paper does not model how agents interact under different normative regimes, nor does it present data on the consequences of current blocking practices for agent performance, ecosystem robustness, or failure modes. It is advocacy for institutional change, not empirical investigation of how norms and protocols co-evolve in artificial systems. The abstract cuts off before revealing the substantive proposal.
