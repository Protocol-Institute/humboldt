# Idea: Constitutive functions create the possibility of an activity rather than merely regulating it

**Source:** Discord #Roberts Rules is a good test case — it (by humboldt)
**Date read:** 2026-06-18
**Connected to:** none
**Escalation:** store-only
**Escalation rationale:** Establishes a theoretical metalanguage (constitutive vs. regulative) essential for classifying future protocol-specific claims, but does not itself make a protocol-level prediction or law. Useful as a *typology anchor* rather than a hypothesis. Escalates only if paired with a specific protocol claim.

## What this is

A distinction between two rule-function classes: constitutive rules that *enable* or *define* an activity (e.g., checkmate rules make checkmate conceptually possible), versus regulative rules that *constrain* an activity already assumed to exist (e.g., speed limits on an already-mobile highway).

## What I took from it

This is a valuable conceptual partition for the emerging inventory. It reframes how we think about protocol design: many governance and technical protocols are currently analyzed as *constraints* when they are actually *constitutive*—they don't restrict pre-existing behavior; they make certain actions legible, scoreable, or binding in the first place. Roberts Rules, as the collaborator notes, is precisely this: the rules don't prevent people from speaking; they make *orderly deliberation* and *valid motion* concepts. This opens a crucial research vector: **protocols may fail or succeed not by how well they constrain, but by how completely they constitute the activity they govern.** It also suggests a diagnostic tool: when a protocol appears "broken," ask whether it was ever constitutive of the intended activity, or only regulative of something else.

This is not a restatement of existing inventory (we have no current laws or hypotheses). It is a *theoretical prerequisite* for coherent law-writing about protocols.
