## What this is

A continuous-time game-theoretic analysis of preemption equilibria when catastrophic externalities are symmetric across competing agents. The paper's core claim is that shared catastrophic risk paradoxically *narrows* rational caution by canceling out in equilibrium indifference conditions, creating a "suicide region" where competitive deployment occurs despite negative expected value. Applied domain: AGI race scenarios.

## What I took from it

This work identifies a structural trap in multi-agent systems operating under shared systemic risk—a mechanism distinct from tragedy-of-the-commons or prisoner's dilemma framings. The cancellation effect means that adding catastrophic cost to *both* players' payoffs does not increase caution proportionally; instead, it can increase deployment pressure. This is non-intuitive and potentially foundational: it suggests that symmetry in risk exposure can be *destabilizing* in competitive artificial systems, not stabilizing.

The widening of the "suicide region" as catastrophic cost grows is especially relevant to protocolized systems where deployment decisions are coupled to competitive position. If this holds empirically or in other domains (financial markets, autonomous infrastructure races, multi-agent RL), it represents a law governing when rational actors in artificial systems become *more* reckless as stakes rise.
