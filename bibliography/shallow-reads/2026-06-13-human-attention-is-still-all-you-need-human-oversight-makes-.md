## What this is

An empirical study of Human-in-the-Loop Economic Research (HLER), a decision architecture designed to make LLM-assisted research reliable through structured cognitive division of labor. The core argument: reliability in AI-assisted research depends not on model capability alone but on *how* cognitive work is distributed between humans and machines, enforced through pre-commitment, decision sequencing, accountability, and attention allocation.

## What I took from it

This work identifies a critical inversion: the bottleneck in AI-assisted research is not reasoning capacity but *governance structure*. Rather than asking "what can models do?" it asks "what institutional arrangements prevent models from breaking research validity?" The HLER framework suggests reliability emerges from sequencing constraints and attention architecture—i.e., forcing human review at specific decision points, locking in choices before model output, and allocating cognitive attention *strategically* rather than uniformly.

This is directly relevant to the study of protocolized systems: it proposes that artificial systems (here, LLM-based research) become reliable not through capability scaling but through *recursive human validation loops* embedded in workflow. The mechanism is architectural, not epistemic. This suggests a class of problems where oversight topology matters more than component intelligence.

## Research connections

- **[Hypothesis: Reliability as protocol property]** HLER treats reliability as a property of decision sequencing and attention allocation, not model training—consistent with the view that artificial systems gain reliability through embedded governance structures.

## Candidate laws or signals

- **CL-HLER-1:** *Reliability in hybrid human-AI systems scales with the specificity of cognitive labor partition and pre-commitment constraints, independent of component capability.*
