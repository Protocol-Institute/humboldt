## What this is

This paper applies Elinor Ostrom's eight design principles for common-pool resource (CPR) governance to "negative commons" — shared burdens or harms rather than shared resources — using e-waste trade and Linux kernel development as case studies. It argues that NCPR governance requires adaptation of Ostrom's framework to address collective management of externalities and liabilities rather than preservation of finite assets.

## What I took from it

The work is primarily **normative and applied**, attempting to retrofit existing institutional theory (Ostrom 1990) onto two heterogeneous domains. The Linux kernel example is interesting as a system exhibiting governance-like structures, but the connection between distributed software governance and e-waste management is tenuous rather than revealing a deep structural homology. The paper does not present a sustained theoretical argument about *why* negative and positive commons might obey different laws, nor does it empirically demonstrate that Ostrom's principles actually fail at NCPR management — it mainly catalogs how they would need "adaptation," which is weaker than mechanism discovery.

The work confirms an intuition that artificial systems (kernel communities, supply chains) exhibit commons dynamics, but it does not advance a testable claim about how protocolized systems *differ* from institutional ones in governance capacity or failure modes.
