## What this is

An economic paper analyzing how philanthropic foundations choose between three commitment structures: termination at a fixed time, perpetual continuation, or discretionary (open) termination. The work frames these choices as trade-offs between organizational flexibility, investment in foundation-specific capital, and protection against principal-agent drift in future governance.

## What I took from it

The paper applies a standard mechanism-design lens to protocol lifespan commitment—a narrower problem than the laws we're tracking. It correctly identifies that perpetual commitment increases sunk capital formation (raising exit costs, consistent with L-001 ossification dynamics), but the analysis remains within classical institutional economics. The framing treats commitment as a *choice variable* rather than as an emergent outcome of adoption pressure, protocol layering, or formalization ratchets.

The work does not investigate whether commitment structures themselves become locked, whether agents strategically *mis-specify* their own termination conditions to constrain successors, or how commitment opacity (concealing true termination conditions) functions as a coordination surface. It also does not engage with how formal legibility of termination rules affects optimization pressure on the boundary itself—a potential vector for L-012 (Intervention-Layer Displacement) or L-014 (Strategic Boundary Concentration).

## Seed

**Seed title:** Commitment Specification as Successor Constraint Lock

**Seed type:** motif

**Seed text:** Organizations that formally specify termination conditions (perpetuity clauses, sunset provisions, discretionary exit rules) may thereby create a stable, low-cost coordination surface for successive governing bodies to maintain the original commitment, even when state-contingent conditions would favor reversal. The more legible and formally encoded the commitment structure, the higher the cost (social, reputational, procedural) for a successor to deviate. This suggests that formalization of organizational lifespan itself functions as an ossification mechanism parallel to protocol formalization—not through adoption pressure, but through deliberate commitment design that shifts the burden of change onto future decision-makers.
