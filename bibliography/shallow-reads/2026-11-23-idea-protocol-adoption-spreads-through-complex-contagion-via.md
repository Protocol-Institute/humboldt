# Idea: Protocol adoption spreads through complex contagion via reinforcement networks rather than broadcast

**Kind:** content  
**Escalation:** store-only

## What this is

Protocol adoption nonmonotonicity arises not from preference volatility but from switching constraints embedded in reinforcement network topology — agents adopt when reinforcement thresholds across peer networks align, not when broadcast signals accumulate.

## What I took from it

This is a **mechanistic refinement of L-010**, not a contradiction. L-010 observes that adoption is nonmonotonic (adoption can reverse or stall despite positive signals); this idea proposes a topological source: reinforcement networks create switching costs and threshold locking that opinion-dynamics models miss. The claim usefully distinguishes between:
- **Broadcast adoption** (threshold = signal strength accumulated over time)
- **Complex contagion adoption** (threshold = alignment of local peer reinforcement across distinct network clusters)

This opens a sharp question: Does the nonmonotonicity in L-010 arise from *preference instability* (agents change their minds) or from *structural lock-in* (agents cannot afford to switch until coordination reaches critical density across their reinforcement peers)? The idea suggests the latter. If true, it redirects investigation away from volatility modeling and toward topology-of-switching-constraints.

It also implies that adoption reversals might be more stable and persistent than opinion-reversal models would predict — because the switching cost resists re-adoption once peers have coordinated elsewhere.

## Seed

**Seed title:** Switching-Topology Nonmonotonicity in Reinforcement-Locked Adoption  
**Seed type:** question + motif  
**Seed text:** Protocol adoption exhibits nonmonotonicity not because agent preferences are volatile but because switching constraints are locked into reinforcement network topology. Agents adopt when coordination alignment reaches critical density *across their reinforcement peers*, not when broadcast signals accumulate. This suggests adoption reversals may be more structurally stable and harder to unwind than opinion-reversal models predict, and that protocol ossification may be driven as much by switching-cost topology as by adoption volume. Under what conditions do reinforcement networks become *opaque* to external interventions, and can such opacity be distinguished from preference-based threshold resistance?
