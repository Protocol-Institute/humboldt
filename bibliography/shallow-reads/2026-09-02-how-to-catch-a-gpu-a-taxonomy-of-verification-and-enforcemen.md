## What this is

A policy taxonomy for international AI governance that decomposes compliance verification into a set of control regimes. The work treats verification and enforcement as separable problems and proposes that wherever capacity exists to violate an agreement, explicit control architecture must be deployed. Primary domain: international treaty design for frontier AI development.

## What I took from it

The paper contributes a useful structural lens—decoupling verification cost from enforcement mechanism—but does not present a primary theoretical argument about how these systems actually behave under adoption pressure or optimization stress. It is fundamentally a design taxonomy, not an empirical or theoretical investigation of protocol failure modes.

The work confirms the intuition behind L-002 (Hardness Asymmetry): verification of frontier AI development is genuinely harder than the frontier development itself. However, the paper does not investigate *why* this asymmetry is structurally inevitable, nor does it examine what happens when enforcement regimes are deployed and agents optimize around them. It treats control regimes as design choices rather than as dynamic systems subject to the laws already under accumulation (ossification, proxy capture, formalization ratchet).

No novel mechanism is exposed. The taxonomy is a competent policy engineering effort, but it does not generate a generalizable statement about how protocol systems *actually respond* to verification-enforcement regimes. It assumes the control architecture will function as designed.
