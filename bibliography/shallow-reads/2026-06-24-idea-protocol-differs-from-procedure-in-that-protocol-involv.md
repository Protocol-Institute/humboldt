# Idea: Protocol differs from procedure in that protocol involves a clerk/attendant that actively expects certain guest moves

**Source:** Discord #I imagine the gap is outline in that ZIP (by 4umd)
**Date read:** 2026-06-24
**Connected to:** none
**Escalation:** store-only
**Escalation rationale:** Restatement of established distinction; no new empirical claims or mechanisms warrant promotion at this time. Useful for clarity but does not extend current law/hypothesis inventory.

## What this is

The claim that protocols are fundamentally *expectation-driven interactions* (where a system or agent anticipates and conditions on guest behavior) whereas procedures are *passive sequential permits* (where steps are allowed but not anticipated).

## What I took from it

This idea restates a foundational distinction already present in the research context—the active vs. passive, anticipatory vs. permissive framing. The metaphor of "clerk/attendant" is evocative but does not introduce new structural criteria: it rephrases what we already mean by protocol as a *relational* system with embedded expectations, versus procedure as a *linear* or *branching* specification.

The idea does *not* clarify:
- How expectations are encoded (rules? learned models? normative signals?)
- What happens when guest behavior *violates* expectation (does the protocol degrade, branch, or escalate?)
- Whether "expectation" is computational, legal, or phenomenological

It is useful for teaching and conceptual hygiene—the attendant/guest framing makes the asymmetry vivid—but it does not open new empirical questions or identify new mechanisms. It strengthens existing conceptual boundaries without disrupting them.

## Research connections

- **Hypothesis (inferred):** Protocols embed relational accountability; procedures do not.

## Candidate laws or signals

**None.** This idea is a conceptual restatement in metaphorical language. To escalate it, we would need: empirical signatures of expectation (behavioral traces, failure modes, cost structures), evidence that the clerk/attendant model predicts outcomes procedures do not, or a formal definition of "expectation" that generates testable differences.
