## What this is

A policy-oriented analysis of structural dependency in frontier AI systems, documenting resource asymmetries (compute capacity, data center distribution) between EU and US/China and evaluating the adequacy of EU regulatory responses. Primary contribution appears to be empirical quantification of geopolitical imbalance rather than theoretical mechanism or generalized pattern.

## What I took from it

The paper frames AI sovereignty as a *structural capacity problem* rather than purely regulatory or institutional one — the 16:1 compute ratio and 85% non-EU hyperscale capacity are presented as hard constraints on autonomy. This is relevant as a diagnostic case study for how protocolized systems amplify existing power asymmetries through capital/infrastructure concentration, but the argument remains domain-specific (frontier AI policy) without attempting to articulate a generalizable mechanism about how dependence emerges in artificial systems more broadly.

The implicit claim — that regulatory frameworks (AI Act, etc.) cannot compensate for fundamental resource gaps — is worth tracking but needs stronger theoretical grounding about *what kinds of dependencies are irreducible* versus which can be overcome through standards, interoperability, or distributed architecture. Currently reads as a well-evidenced complaint rather than a law candidate.
