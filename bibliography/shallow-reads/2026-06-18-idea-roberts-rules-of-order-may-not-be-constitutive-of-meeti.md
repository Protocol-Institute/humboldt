# Idea: Roberts Rules of Order may not be constitutive of meetings and decisions, since meetings and decisions can occur without them

**Source:** Discord #Roberts Rules is a good test case — it (by plague_year)
**Date read:** 2026-06-18
**Connected to:** none
**Escalation:** store-only
**Escalation rationale:** Empirical counterexample testing the constitutive framework; valuable boundary probe but does not yet warrant hypothesis candidacy without positive law candidate to pair against.

## What this is

A challenge to the claim that formalized procedural protocols (Roberts Rules of Order) are *necessary* for the existence of meetings and decisions, using the counterexample that meetings and binding decisions demonstrably occur in contexts that ignore or reject Roberts Rules entirely.

## What I took from it

This idea correctly identifies a category error risk in the constitutive framework: the distinction between *enabling conditions* and *regulative conditions*. Roberts Rules may regulate *how* meetings proceed, structure their legitimacy, or reduce friction—but the empirical fact that meetings happen without them suggests they are regulative, not constitutive in the strict sense (i.e., not the generative condition for the phenomenon itself).

This sharpens a critical question for the research: **What actually is constitutive vs. regulative in protocolized systems?** A protocol might be constitutive *within a specific institutional context* (e.g., Roberts Rules constitutes parliamentary legitimacy in legislatures) while remaining purely regulative elsewhere (e.g., a casual group chat "follows" Roberts Rules informally, if at all, and meetings still occur). The claim opens the possibility that **constitutivity is context-bound and role-dependent**, not universal to the protocol itself.

This does not overturn constitutivity as a concept—it refines it. The idea warns against treating all protocols as equally constitutive.

## Research connections

- **[Constitutive function framework]:** Tests boundary; suggests protocols may be regulative rather than constitutive in many cases, or constitutive only *within specific institutional frames*.
