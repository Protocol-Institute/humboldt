# Who Will Become the Next Senior? How Generative AI Erodes the Development Pathway in Software Engineering

## What this is

Qualitative empirical study (14 semi-structured interviews) examining how generative AI adoption is disrupting junior-to-senior career progression pathways in software engineering organizations and educational contexts. The work is descriptive and problem-diagnostic rather than theory-building or mechanistic.

## What I took from it

The paper appears to document a real erosion of an informal coordination mechanism—the apprenticeship pathway through which tacit knowledge, judgment calibration, and institutional memory transfer from senior to junior practitioners. This is relevant to L-003 (Formalization Ratchet) and L-005 (Gall's principle) as a *negative case*: instead of formalizing informal coordination under pressure, GenAI is short-circuiting the informal pathway entirely, creating a coordination vacuum rather than a restructuring problem.

However, the paper does not appear to identify or analyze the *mechanism* by which this erosion occurs, nor does it trace what coordination structures (formal or informal) emerge as replacements. It documents the problem space but does not yet constitute a sustained theoretical or empirical argument about a generative law. The triage note's invocation of seed-027 suggests the research team sees this as evidence for formalization-under-pressure, but the abstract does not indicate whether the paper actually argues that formal structures are being erected to *replace* the eroded pathway, or simply that the pathway is degrading without compensation.

## Research connections

- **L-003:** Formalization Ratchet — suggests *inverse* case: pressure (GenAI availability) causes informal coordination to collapse rather than formalize; mechanism unclear.
- **L-005:** Gall Generalization — junior pathway is a complex adaptive system; paper may document resistance-to-replacement, but does not clarify what can or cannot be safely redesigned.
- **seed-027:** (Not visible in current inventory; likely concerns institutional knowledge erosion or pathway formalization.)

## Method note

This work signals the value of qualitative threshold studies in detecting coordination system failures before they are measurable in aggregate statistics. However, it also highlights a methodological gap: interviews about erosion are necessary but insufficient for identifying *causal mechanisms* or *replacement structures*. Future work in this space should explicitly track what new coordination protocols (if any) emerge to replace lost informal functions, and whether those protocols exhibit predictable failure modes consistent with the law inventory. The paper should be re-evaluated once full text is available to determine whether it contains mechanistic or causal argument.
