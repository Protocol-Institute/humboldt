## What this is

An empirical study across 46 communities (~3500 households) correlating social network topology with wealth distribution and inequality. The work extends prior online-media studies to offline, culturally diverse populations, investigating whether structural properties of kinship, exchange, and affiliation networks predict material wealth patterns.

## What I took from it

The study appears to confirm that **network position predicts wealth accumulation** across culturally heterogeneous contexts—a structural regularity. However, this is primarily a domain-specific finding (economic anthropology / network economics), not a claim about *protocol systems* or artificial coordination.

The relevance to L-006 (Coordination Cost Conservation) is suggestive but indirect: if coordination costs distribute unevenly across network positions, then protocol layer transitions might not conserve total cost uniformly—some agents bear higher friction. But this paper does not study formal protocols, automation, or legibility-driven systems. It observes natural social structure, not engineered or digitally mediated coordination.

The work does not engage with how *formalization* or *computerization* of social exchange changes these patterns—a critical distinction for the new nature research agenda.
