# Not All That Can Be Automated Should Be Automated: Strategic Minimalism as a Disciplined and Ethically Grounded Approach to AI Adoption

**Kind:** meta

## What this is

A normative position paper arguing for restraint in AI adoption based on virtue ethics, bounded rationality, and proportionality rather than capability-driven deployment. The work frames technological restraint as a strategic discipline rather than obstacle, positioned against organizational culture that treats automation as synonymous with progress.

## What I took from it

This is a prescriptive intervention in adoption dynamics, not an empirical or theoretical characterization of protocol ossification. It advocates *for* the conditions that might *produce* ossification (selective formalization, preserved informal structures, slower adoption cycles) but does not present evidence that such approaches prevent or delay the mechanisms described in L-001, nor does it model the cost-shifting that L-006 predicts when coordination is deliberately kept informal.

The paper does not challenge the claim that adoption pressure drives ossification; it argues adoption pressure itself should be resisted on ethical grounds. This is orthogonal to the causal claim we're testing. The framing of "restraint as strategy" may be interesting for understanding *why* some organizations resist the adoption-ossification cycle, but the paper appears to treat adoption velocity as a choice variable under organizational control, not as a structural consequence of protocol function and competitive pressure.

## Research connections

- **L-001:** Notes adoption pressure but advocates resistance; does not model whether this resistance postpones or prevents ossification, or merely delays it at a coordination cost.
- **L-006:** Strategic minimalism would preserve informality; relevant to whether coordination costs are conserved or genuinely reduced under selective formalization.
- **seed-144:** Informality as coordination cost refuge under substitution pressure — aligned but not tested empirically.

## Method note

This exemplifies the risk of normative framing colonizing empirical inquiry. The paper treats "should" (ethical prescription) as if it answers "will" (causal mechanism). For this research agenda, we need to separate the claim that organizations *ought* to adopt minimally from the claim that they *can* do so sustainably, and from the claim that doing so alters the underlying protocol dynamics. The work would be stronger if it modeled the cost structure of restraint rather than simply advocating it. Meta-observation: prescriptive papers on technology governance are valuable for identifying *friction points* in adoption, but should be read as data about organizational intent, not mechanism.
