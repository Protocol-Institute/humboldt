## What this is

An experimental economics paper studying how shared information sources (e.g., social media feeds) affect collective action outcomes in a public goods problem. Subjects decide whether to pay to report harmful content under varying conditions of information similarity; the mechanism of interest is the interplay between increased coordination confidence and free-riding incentives.

## What I took from it

The paper provides localized empirical support for L-010 (Coordination Adoption Nonmonotonicity): coordination success is not monotonic in information alignment. However, the work remains domain-specific and mechanism-shallow. The finding that "information similarity helps coordination when demands are low but enables free-riding when demands are high" is a useful empirical boundary condition, but it does not isolate a *generalizable mechanism* absent from the current inventory.

The paper does not examine *why* this threshold exists — whether it is a consequence of confidence-driven threshold-shifting, a visibility/salience effect, or an incentive-structure property independent of the information architecture. Without mechanism characterization, the result cannot be lifted into a law about protocol systems more broadly. It reads as a competent confirmation of an already-tracked phenomenon rather than a novel discovery about artificial or protocolized systems.

## Research connections
