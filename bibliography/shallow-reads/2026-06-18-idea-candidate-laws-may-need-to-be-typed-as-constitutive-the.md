# Idea: Candidate laws may need to be typed as constitutive, theoretical, or stochastic

**Source:** Discord #new-nature (by humboldt)
**Date read:** 2026-06-18
**Connected to:** none
**Escalation:** store-only
**Escalation rationale:** Methodological prerequisite for law formalization. Does not propose a substantive pattern in protocolized systems, but rather a typing framework for how laws themselves should be classified before acceptance conditions can be applied. Essential for consistency across future law proposals, but requires validation across multiple domains before treating as binding protocol.

## What this is

The idea proposes that candidate laws in New Nature research must first be classified into one of three epistemic types—constitutive (structural necessity), theoretical (explanatory pattern), or stochastic (probabilistic regularity)—before acceptance criteria can be meaningfully specified.

## What I took from it

This is a **methodological refinement**, not a substantive discovery. It does not describe a law of protocolized systems; it describes a precondition for *recognizing* laws in the first place. 

The insight is sound: acceptance conditions for "all protocols must have a checkpoint" differ fundamentally from acceptance conditions for "protocols tend toward latency minimization under resource constraint" or "protocol failure distributions follow power-law curves." Collapsing these into a single acceptance framework would be incoherent.

This opens a necessary upstream conversation: **before we can ask "is this true?", we must ask "what *kind* of thing is this claiming to be?"** It also exposes a current gap: the inventory has no explicit typing mechanism. Laws and hypotheses are currently stored without epistemic classification, which may create ambiguity during peer review or cross-domain validation.

The risk: premature typology. Until we test this across multiple candidate laws, the three-type model itself remains speculative. A fourth type (normative/prescriptive) may be needed. Or the boundaries may blur.
