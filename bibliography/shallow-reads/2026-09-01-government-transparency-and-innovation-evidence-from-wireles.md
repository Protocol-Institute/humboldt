## What this is

An econometric study using a 1998 quasi-experiment in wireless technology disclosure to measure how government transparency affects innovation adoption rates. The paper quantifies a causal effect: publication of technical details online increased follow-on technology use from 0.2 to 1.36 products within five years, with effects concentrated among competitors and foreign firms.

## What I took from it

This is a clean causal identification of a *transparency shock* — but it is documenting acceleration of adoption, not the ossification, paradigm-locking, or resistance mechanisms that animate L-001 and L-013. The wireless domain is relatively young (1998) and pre-paradigm-locked; firms are in rapid exploration mode, not defending entrenched coordination structures. The paper shows that visibility into technical detail *lowers* barriers to imitation and recombination — a straightforward information-availability effect.

The relevant question for L-001 and L-013 is whether this acceleration *also* accelerates the conditions under which ossification or paradigm-locking then sets in. Does transparency-driven rapid adoption of a technical approach create the adoption pressure that later triggers L-001's resistance to modification? The paper does not examine post-adoption lock-in, only the adoption phase itself. It is a useful control case — showing that transparency *can* accelerate adoption under low-lock-in conditions — but does not test whether the same transparency creates the conditions for later ossification.

## Research connections

- **L-001:** Documents a case where adoption *does* accelerate under transparency; question remains whether this same acceleration load triggers downstream ossification under higher coordination pressure.
- **L-013:** No direct connection; the wireless domain in 1998 shows no evidence of paradigm-locked anomaly tolerance because the paradigm itself was still fluid.
- **seed-054 (verification-cost-collapse-value-collapse):** Tangentially relevant; transparency reduces the cost of verifying what a technology does, which may trigger value collapse if the verification reveals commodification.
