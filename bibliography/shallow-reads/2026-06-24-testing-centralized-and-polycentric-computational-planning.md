## What this is

A benchmark paper comparing three organizational protocols for resource allocation—centralized computational planning, decentralized agent-based markets, and hybrid meta-markets—within a synthetic economy with realistic friction (input-output networks, capacity constraints, endogenous prices, shocks). The central empirical finding is that the centralized planner consistently outperforms decentralized alternatives on welfare loss metrics across multiple test conditions.

## What I took from it

This is a tool/benchmark contribution rather than a primary theoretical or mechanistic investigation. It tests *performance* of known protocols under controlled conditions but does not interrogate *why* centralized planning wins, what information structures enable or constrain each approach, or what conditions flip the ranking. The paper's framing as a "reproducible synthetic benchmark" signals it is designed for comparative evaluation, not for discovering new laws governing protocol behavior.

The finding itself—that computational planning beats markets on this metric—aligns with classical optimization theory but does not establish a generalizable law about when centralization becomes superior in protocolized systems. The absence of information-theoretic analysis, mechanism design rigor, or investigation into phase transitions or scaling limits leaves the result domain-bound (a specific economy structure under specific shock profiles).
