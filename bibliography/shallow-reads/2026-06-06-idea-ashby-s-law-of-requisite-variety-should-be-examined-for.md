## What this is

The proposal that Ashby's law of requisite variety—the principle that a regulator must possess at least as much variety (complexity) as the system it regulates—may yield a distinct corollary when applied to protocol-mediated and decentralized artificial systems.

## What I took from it

This is a generative observation rather than a fully articulated claim. Ashby's law is well-established in classical cybernetics and control theory; applying it to protocolized systems is not novel per se, but the *corollary* framing suggests the collaborator suspects that protocol environments create emergent regulatory dynamics that cannot be captured by Ashby's original formulation alone. 

The idea opens several lines: (1) Do decentralized or consensus-driven regulators operate under different variety constraints than centralized controllers? (2) Can a protocol's rule-set serve as a form of "requisite variety" that distributes regulatory capacity across many actors? (3) Does the inscriptability of rules in code create a new relationship between system complexity and regulatory complexity? These are worth exploring, but the claim needs specification: what corollary, exactly, and in what regime does it hold?

## Research connections

- **Ashby's Law (classical):** A baseline for comparing how protocol-mediated systems either satisfy or evade classical requisite variety requirements.

## Candidate laws or signals

**CL-vgr-001 (candidate for development):** *Protocol-distributed systems may satisfy requisite variety through inscribed rule-variety rather than actor-variety alone; the diversity of valid state-transitions encoded in consensus rules may substitute for or amplify the regulatory capacity of individual nodes.*

This is **not yet a law** — it requires: (a) formal definition of "rule-variety" vs. Ashby's actor-variety, (b) empirical or formal demonstration that it holds in named protocol systems, (c) specification of when/whether it breaks down.
