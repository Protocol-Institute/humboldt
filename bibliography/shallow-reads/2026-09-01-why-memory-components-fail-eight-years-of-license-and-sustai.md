## What this is

An empirical inventory of failure modes in open-source infrastructure projects (105 projects, 38 events over 8 years), arguing that governance structure, capital model, and licensing posture function as architectural constraints on technical system resilience. The work challenges the treatment of "memory components" as purely technical objects by documenting how institutional collapse correlates with technical adoption bottlenecks.

## What I took from it

This is a methodological contribution that reframes infrastructure failure as a governance-embedded problem rather than a technical one. The observation that major architectural surveys omit capital and licensing variables is itself significant: it suggests the field has compartmentalized "social" and "technical" architecture, missing their deep coupling. The 24% adverse-event rate across a constructed sample is a useful baseline, though the paper appears to be correlational rather than causal.

For the research agenda, this complicates L-001 (Protocol Ossification) and L-005 (Gall's Principle) by introducing a hidden variable: ossification may be partly driven not by adoption pressure on the protocol itself, but by institutional brittleness in the governance substrate. Seed-027 (Planck Principle: Institutional Memory) appears directly relevant—the paper seems to argue that technical systems cannot outlive the institutional memory and capital structures that birthed them, suggesting memory failure is substrate-level rather than algorithmic.

## Research connections

- **L-001:** Ossification under adoption may reflect capital-structure rigidity, not just protocol lock-in.
- **L-005:** Working systems that resist restructuring may be locked by governance debt, not technical complexity alone.
- **seed-027:** Technical memory components may be constrained by institutional memory half-life and capital discontinuity.

## Method note

This work demonstrates the value of constructing long-panel datasets across institutional and technical variables simultaneously, rather than treating them as separate explanatory domains. The choice to treat governance and capital as *architectural variables* (not confounds) suggests that future law-building work should incorporate institutional history as a primary data stream, not a contextual footnote. The correlation between license events and technical adoption suggests the need for fine-grained event catalogs that track institutional ruptures alongside technical milestones—a genre of work the field currently underproduces.
