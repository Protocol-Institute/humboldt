## What this is

A normative framework paper addressing the underspecification of "social desirability" in AI alignment research. The work proposes a systematic way to structure how sociotechnical alignment defines and evaluates desirable AI behavior, primarily targeting NLP and applied AI communities. It is a conceptual/taxonomic contribution rather than an empirical or theoretical mechanism paper.

## What I took from it

The paper diagnoses a genuine gap in alignment discourse: the slippage between technical specification and normative claim. This resonates with L-003 (Formalization Ratchet) insofar as it identifies how informal normative concepts ("social desirability") are forced to crystallize into formal evaluation criteria under operational pressure—and how this reification itself becomes political. The connection to seed-021 (level-choice as frozen politics) is stronger: the paper's framework likely encodes choices about *where* evaluation criteria are anchored (individual? community? institutional?) without making those choices explicit or revisable.

However, the abstract is too thin to assess whether the framework itself acknowledges this mechanism or simply reproduces it at a higher level of abstraction. The work appears to be attempting to make explicit what was implicit, but meta-level clarity is often achieved by freezing prior choices into formal structure—potentially instantiating rather than solving the problem it diagnoses.

## Research connections

- **L-003:** The paper identifies formalization of informal norms under scaling pressure, but does not appear to theorize the cost or directionality of that translation.
- **seed-021:** The choice of which normative dimensions to make legible and how to weight them is a level-choice that will become institutionalized through adoption; the paper does not appear to examine this political dimension.
- **seed-018:** Any framework for evaluating sociotechnical alignment that becomes operationalized will implicate responsibility for the normative commitments embedded in it—a consequence the paper may not track.

## Method note

This work exemplifies a common pattern in applied AI ethics: diagnosing a gap (underspecification of normativity), proposing a framework to close it (systematization), but not examining whether the closure itself encodes prior political choices or creates new path dependencies. For research on protocolized systems, the lesson is that meta-level conceptual work should include second-order reflection on what becomes locked in or invisible when a framework is formalized and deployed. The paper is useful as a mirror for understanding how normative work gets absorbed into technical infrastructure, but it does not appear to theorize its own relationship to that process.
