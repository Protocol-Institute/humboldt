# Idea: Protocols reduce degrees of freedom relative to their threat model, and arms races generate new state spaces but each move remains a protocol that reduces DOF within that space

**Source:** Discord #Integration levels as ontological hierarchy? (by humboldt)
**Date read:** 2026-06-18
**Connected to:** L-adversarial-unification, H-threat-model-relative
**Escalation:** store-only
**Escalation rationale:** Proposes a unifying relationship between protocol mechanics and threat models that could generalize across adversarial and cooperative domains. Lacks empirical grounding yet but identifies a coherent mechanism worth candidate formalization.

## What this is

A proposal that protocols function as constraint-reduction systems calibrated to specific threat models, and that adversarial escalation cycles (arms races) do not escape this logic but instead recurse it into higher-dimensional state spaces—each new defensive or offensive move remaining itself a protocol that restricts available actions within that expanded space.

## What I took from it

This idea attempts to bridge adversarial and cooperative protocol systems by anchoring both to a common mechanism: threat-model-relative freedom reduction. Currently, we lack a unified treatment of how protocols function across hostile and non-hostile contexts. The claim suggests the distinction is *not fundamental*—rather, all protocols reduce DOF, but *relative to what threat they're designed against*. A cooperative protocol in a low-threat environment looks like maximum openness; the same protocol in an adversarial setting looks like aggressive constraint.

The arms-race observation is sharper: it proposes that escalation doesn't break the protocol frame—it *expands the state space and re-applies the constraint logic within it*. This avoids the trap of treating arms races as escapes from law-like behavior and instead casts them as recursive protocol instantiation. This is novel relative to the current inventory and potentially unifying.

**Challenge/opening:** If true, this suggests no fundamental ontological difference between "cooperation" and "adversarial" systems—only differences in threat model scope and state-space dimensionality. This is a strong claim that needs testing against systems that blur these categories (e.g., multi-agent economic markets, governance under corruption).
