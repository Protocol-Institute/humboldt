## What this is

A large-scale empirical survey of occupational requirements and task specifications extracted from Chinese job postings (750M records, monthly cadence). The paper argues that occupations are defined not by title stability but by the dynamic shifting of entry barriers and task composition—and that job ads serve as a real-time instrument for detecting labor market formalization pressure and occupational redefinition.

## What I took from it

The paper documents a concrete mechanism supporting L-003 (The Formalization Ratchet): occupational definitions are being progressively *narrowed and credentialized* under scaling and regulatory pressure. The example of "accountant" at small vs. listed firms shows how the same nominal role bifurcates into two protocols with different verification requirements (certification vs. informality). 

However, the paper itself is primarily a methodological and descriptive contribution—it builds an instrument for measuring occupational drift, not a theory of *why* drift happens or under what conditions it reverses. The data likely contains evidence for L-003 (formalization under stress), but the paper does not sustain a causal argument about protocol ossification or coordination cost conservation. It observes the symptom (task creep, credential inflation, requirement drift) without proposing mechanism or generative law. The work is empirically grounded but analytically shallow on the theoretical side.

## Research connections

- **L-003:** Confirms the phenomenon (requirement formalization under scaling pressure) but does not develop mechanism or cross-domain generalization.
- **seed-020:** Symptom hierarchy displacement in occupational definition aligns with the bifurcation pattern observed, but the paper does not explain why displacement occurs or predict downstream effects.
