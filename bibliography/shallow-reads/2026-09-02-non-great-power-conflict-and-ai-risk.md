## What this is

A policy-framing paper that challenges the assumption that great power conflict poses the dominant AI risk vector, arguing instead that non-great-power conflict (NGPC)—civil wars, proxy wars, asymmetric conflicts—deserves equal analytic weight. The work is primarily a conceptual reorientation and risk taxonomy rather than a sustained theoretical or empirical argument about protocol dynamics.

## What I took from it

The paper reframes the deployment incentive landscape by relocating catastrophic risk from symmetric racing conditions (GPC) to asymmetric, fragmented conflict environments. This is relevant to L-009 (Catastrophic Risk Cancellation in Symmetric Racing Protocols) as a *counterexample or qualification*: if deployment prizes are distributed across many smaller, uncoordinated actors in NGPC contexts, the symmetric racing assumption collapses. However, the paper does not model protocol dynamics, adoption cascades, or the mechanisms by which distributed incentive structures alter optimization pressure. It is a domain-expansion argument, not a mechanism discovery.

The framing does highlight a gap: current laws in the inventory assume either centralized deployment (L-001, L-007) or symmetric bilateral racing (L-009). NGPC scenarios introduce *polycentric, asymmetric adoption* where verification costs, trust accumulation, and coordination norms behave differently. But the paper itself does not develop these dynamics—it leaves them as open research questions.

## Seed

**Seed title:** Deployment Incentive Polycentralization Under Conflict Fragmentation

**Seed type:** observation

**Seed text:** In conflict environments where deployment authority is fragmented across many uncoordinated actors (civil wars, proxy wars, nonstate agents), the optimization pressure on safety-critical protocols shifts from symmetric racing (first-mover advantage concentrated) to *asynchronous opportunistic adoption*—each actor independently optimizes given local constraints and incomplete information about other actors' moves. This may lower the effective deployment threshold (no single actor needs to solve the hard problem; partial solutions suffice), eliminate mutual restraint mechanisms, and prevent the formation of shared verification norms. The pattern suggests that catastrophic risk in protocol systems may be *inversely* related to the centralization of deployment authority, not directly to the level of conflict intensity.
