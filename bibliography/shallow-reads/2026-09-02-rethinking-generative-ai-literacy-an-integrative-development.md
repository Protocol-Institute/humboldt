## What this is

A position paper proposing a normative educational framework for GenAI teacher competency in K-12 settings. The work diagnoses a "GenAI literacy lag" — the observation that deployment velocity outpaces institutional and pedagogical readiness — and argues for integrating ethics, equity, and agency as constitutive rather than supplementary design commitments in teacher education curricula.

## What I took from it

The paper identifies a real temporal misalignment between protocol adoption (GenAI in classrooms) and the formal institutional structures meant to govern its use (teacher training, curriculum design, ethical frameworks). This is a *symptom* of L-003 (Formalization Ratchet) and L-015 (Interpretive Continuity Decay), not a primary source building evidence *for* those laws. The authors document the gap but do not mechanically analyze *why* informal norms fail to keep pace with automation, nor do they present empirical data on how institutions actually respond once pressure mounts.

The paper's core contribution is diagnostic and prescriptive (how to redesign teacher education), not theoretical or mechanistic. It does not explain the generative process by which literacy lags emerge, nor does it provide a replicable model of when formal intervention succeeds or fails at closing such gaps. It reads as advocacy for a particular pedagogical stance within an acknowledged crisis, not as an investigation of the underlying coordination or formalization dynamics.

## Research connections

- **L-003:** The paper names the phenomenon (formalization lag) but does not test conditions under which informal coordination breaks down or when formalization itself becomes a barrier to adaptation.
- **L-015:** Touches on institutional memory and interpretive continuity in distributed educational governance, but focuses on curriculum design rather than evidence of how institutional knowledge decays under rapid protocol change.

## Method note

This paper exemplifies a common pattern in policy-adjacent research: identifying a real coordination failure (literacy lag) but stopping at diagnosis and prescription rather than mechanistic explanation. For our agenda, the gap is instructive: the authors implicitly assume that better normative frameworks will close adoption-readiness gaps, but do not investigate whether formalization itself might *deepen* the lag (e.g., by ossifying pedagogy faster than it can adapt, or by displacing practical wisdom into metric-legible competencies). Future work on L-003 and L-015 should deliberately seek cases where normative intervention fails or backfires, not just cases where it succeeds.
