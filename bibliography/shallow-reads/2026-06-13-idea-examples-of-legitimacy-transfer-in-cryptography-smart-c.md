## What this is

Technical systems gain adoption velocity and institutional credibility by borrowing nomenclature and conceptual frames from pre-existing, ratified domains (law, everyday objects), creating false or partial homology that accelerates user onboarding and regulatory tolerance.

## What I took from it

This is a useful **observational anchor** for a broader pattern we're tracking around legitimacy asymmetry in protocolized systems, but it remains descriptive rather than mechanistic. The examples work because they illustrate *semantic colonization*—technical artifacts reshaping themselves linguistically to occupy slots in human/institutional cognition that already carry trust debt.

The risk is treating this as a discovery when it's better characterized as a **symptom of adoption friction**. The real generative question isn't "why do systems borrow names?" but rather: *what conditions make borrowed legitimacy stick versus collapse?* (A "smart contract" that behaves unlike any legal contract eventually loses its name-power; a "wallet" that violates wallet affordances generates UX resistance.) This points toward an underlying **legitimacy-coherence principle**: borrowed authority decays proportional to deviation from the source domain's behavioral schema.

The cryptography examples are strong because the domains are adjacent (contract to code execution, wallet to key storage), but they're also *limits cases*—what happens when the mapping is more distant?
