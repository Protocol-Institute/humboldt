## What this is

A theoretical paper reframing AI governance around distributed cognition systems rather than bounded agents. The core claim is that institutional capacity to *audit and interpret* a system's operation becomes intractable once cognition fragments across models, tools, humans, storage layers, and authorization boundaries—the "khipu problem" of losing the ability to read the record after the fact.

## What I took from it

This work identifies a genuine structural problem in protocolized systems that governance frameworks (and our own research) have undertheorized. The shift from "what did the system do?" to "can institutions still read what the system was?" reframes the problem space: it's not primarily about individual component behavior or transparency, but about whether distributed decision-making leaves *any legible trace* once delegation chains solidify.

The khipu metaphor is apt—the Inca kept records in knotted cords, legible only to trained readers; when that institutional competence disappeared, the record remained but became noise. The paper appears to argue this is not a contingent failure mode but a structural consequence of distributing cognition. If correct, this has implications beyond AI: it suggests that all sufficiently distributed protocolized systems face a latency problem between operation and institutional reconstruction.

## Candidate laws or signals

- **CL-2606.12414-1:** Institutional legibility degrades non-linearly with distributed cognition; systems that fragment decision-making across n+1 components lose auditability not at rate n but at rate approaching 2^n or worse, once delegation chains and context dependencies interlock.

- **CL-2606.12414-2:** The "khipu threshold"—beyond some complexity in distributed cognition, the cost of maintaining real-time legibility exceeds the cost of operating the system itself; institutions then choose post-hoc reconstruction over live transparency, accepting permanent information loss.
