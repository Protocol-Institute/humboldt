## What this is

A systematic review synthesizing emerging governance literature on autonomous agent systems, positioned as a methodological and conceptual framework rather than a mechanism-level investigation. The paper identifies distinctions between agentic and non-agentic AI and proposes governance categories, but does not present sustained empirical or theoretical argument for a novel mechanism.

## What I took from it

This is a scoping and literature-consolidation exercise, not a primary source. It maps the governance problem space for agentic systems but operates at the level of *naming* distinctions (what makes agentic AI different) rather than *mechanizing* them (how those differences alter incentive structure, protocol dynamics, or failure modes). 

The implicit framing—that agentic systems warrant *different* governance—suggests sensitivity to L-012 (Intervention-Layer Displacement) and seed-021 (level-choice as frozen politics): the choice of governance level and formalization strategy becomes consequential once agents can autonomously interpret and optimize around protocol boundaries. However, the paper does not develop this into a mechanism claim or test it empirically. It remains a governance *taxonomy* problem, not yet a law discovery problem.

## Research connections

- **L-012:** The shift from generative to agentic systems is precisely a case where "prediction" (model output) becomes a "legible input to a decision protocol" (autonomous action)—but this paper does not mechanize the resulting optimization pressure displacement.
- **seed-021:** The paper's framing of governance categories implicitly freezes certain level choices (agent-level vs. infrastructure-level vs. outcome-level control); it does not interrogate how this choice becomes politically sunk.
- **seed-049:** Agentic systems may decouple consensus reasoning from actual behavior; the paper identifies this tension but does not theorize its stability conditions or phase transitions.

## Method note

This paper exemplifies the governance field's tendency to move from problem *description* to *framework* without intermediate mechanistic grounding. For the new nature research agenda, this suggests: (1) scoping reviews are valuable for identifying where mechanism-level investigation is missing, and (2) governance papers should be evaluated on whether they *name* a problem or *explain* why a problem *emerges under specific protocol conditions*. This paper does the former; it is useful as a map of what is not yet mechanized, rather than as evidence for any law.
