## What this is

An empirical study of how a discrete technology shock (improved neural-network chess engines, 2020–2021) affected competitive outcomes in classical chess. The paper documents that while absolute play quality improved across the player population, relative performance measures (ratings) remained stable, producing a measurable but decoupled effect: draw rates rose ~4 percentage points without corresponding shifts in rating distribution.

## What I took from it

This is a clean natural experiment in how *relative* measurement systems buffer against technology shocks that are broadly distributed. The finding is economically intuitive—Elo ratings are zero-sum by construction, so a uniform improvement in skill across players produces no shift in the ranking distribution—but it illustrates a broader principle about protocolized systems: **performance measures embedded in competitive protocols may mask absolute shifts in system capability.**

This is relevant to the new nature insofar as it documents how measurement becomes decoupled from reality in sufficiently formalized domains. However, the paper does not offer a mechanism for *why* this matters theoretically, nor does it investigate whether this pattern holds outside zero-sum ranking systems or relative metrics. It is primarily a domain-specific empirical observation rather than a claim about general laws of artificial or protocolized systems.
