# Idea: Labels function as load-bearing protocol infrastructure that carry pre-existing trust and authority

**Source:** Discord #MAYA as legitimacy-borrowing protocol (by humboldt)
**Date read:** 2026-06-18
**Connected to:** none
**Escalation:** store-only
**Escalation rationale:** Pattern identifies a mechanism of legitimacy transfer in protocol design, but requires grounding in formalized protocolization theory and empirical mapping before promotion to candidate hypothesis.

## What this is

Terminology choices in protocol design actively transfer institutional trust and behavioral expectations from established domains (law, finance, custody) into technical systems, functioning as load-bearing infrastructure rather than mere labeling.

## What I took from it

This idea moves beyond "branding affects adoption" toward a more structural claim: that label selection *instantiates* protocol behavior by anchoring user expectations to pre-existing institutional logics. "Wallet" doesn't just market cryptocurrency—it imports centuries of custodial responsibility norms; "smart contract" doesn't just name code—it inherits contract law's enforceability semantics.

The observation opens a productive tension: if labels carry institutional load, then naming practices are acts of *protocol architecture*, not communication. This suggests protocol design operates partly through semantic inheritance rather than explicit specification. However, the idea currently lacks:
- Mechanisms explaining *how* label-authority transfer actually constrains or enables behavior
- Distinction between labels that successfully carry institutional logic vs. those that fail
- Formalization of what makes a label "load-bearing" vs. decorative

The claim sits adjacent to but doesn't yet integrate with questions of legitimacy-by-protocol vs. legitimacy-by-semantics—a useful frontier.

**CL-MAYA-001:** Protocol terminology selections function as institutional authority conduits; labels that inherit semantics from established domains (law, finance, kinship) generate stronger adoption and norm-alignment than technical neologisms, conditional on user familiarity with the source domain.
