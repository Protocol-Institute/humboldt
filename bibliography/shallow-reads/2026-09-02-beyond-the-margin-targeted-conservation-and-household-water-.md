## What this is

A pre-registered field experiment testing whether end-use targeted conservation interventions (shower timers, water-saving fixtures, real-time feedback) reduce aggregate household water demand in Finland. The work uses utility meters and shower-level instrumentation to measure whether legible behavioral nudges at a specific use-point translate into system-level conservation.

## What I took from it

This is a competent empirical test of a narrow hypothesis about behavioral response to targeted feedback, but it does not sustain a theoretical or mechanism-level argument about protocol systems. The relevant pattern — that making a specific behavior legible and actionable can displace rather than reduce aggregate consumption — is hinted at in the design but not the focus. 

The paper sits squarely in the applied policy evaluation domain: it asks "do showers timers work?" not "what happens to coordination and optimization pressure when you render one node of a resource system precisely measurable?" This is a case study, not an argument about how systems transform under legibility. The absence of evidence for downstream effects (rebound, substitution, or gaming) would be interesting if analyzed as a negative result about L-008 (proxy optimization under computable enforcement), but the paper does not frame or theorize that absence.

## Research connections

- **L-004 [Goodhart Generalization]:** Potential instance — if shower-level metrics displace or partially capture aggregate demand reduction — but paper appears designed to measure end-use savings, not metric capture dynamics.
- **L-008 [Proxy Optimization Under Computable Enforcement]:** Suggestive setup (computable shower-level signals as enforcement proxy), but no mechanism analysis of how agents optimize around or within the legible boundary.
- **seed-077 [Metric-Induced Preference Ratcheting]:** If real-time feedback shifts preference structure over time, data might speak to this, but abstract does not signal that depth of analysis.

**Disposition:** This is a well-scoped experimental economics paper that tests a conservation intervention. It will generate a valid policy effect size. But it does not investigate the *transformation of the system under legibility* — it measures a local behavioral response. Store and monitor for results; escalate only if the paper reports counterintuitive aggregate dynamics (e.g., conservation in target use offset by increased consumption elsewhere, or evidence that feedback drives metric-gaming rather than genuine conservation). As written, it belongs in the applied water policy literature, not the protocolized systems inventory.
