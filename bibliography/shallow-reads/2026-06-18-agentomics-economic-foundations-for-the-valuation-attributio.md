## What this is

A game-theoretic and microeconomic framework for valuing agentic AI systems as productive agents within organizational workflows, moving beyond isolated technical benchmarking to integrated economic contribution measurement. The work treats human-AI workflows as multi-agent economic systems and proposes mechanisms for attribution and pricing.

## What I took from it

The paper addresses a real gap: most AI evaluation is task-centric rather than workflow-systemic. However, the approach appears to be primarily an *application* of established economic tools (attribution, Shapley-like value decomposition, cost accounting) to a new domain rather than a fundamental discovery about how artificial systems behave or organize themselves. The framework treats AI agents as already-intelligible economic actors and focuses on *measurement problems* rather than emergent properties or failure modes unique to protocolized systems.

The work does not appear to investigate whether human-AI workflows exhibit novel economic laws (e.g., phase transitions in coordination cost, scaling inversions, or system brittleness patterns) that would differ from human-only or purely automated systems. It remains instrumentalist: how to price what we already know, not what new dynamics emerge when human and artificial agents are tightly coupled.
