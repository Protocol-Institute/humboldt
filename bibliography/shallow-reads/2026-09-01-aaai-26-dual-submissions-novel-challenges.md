## What this is

A meta-analysis of dual submission practices in peer review, documenting a failure mode in academic publication protocols. The AAAI organizers conducted a comparative audit across nine overlapping archival venues to quantify and characterize simultaneous submissions without disclosure, treating the phenomenon as a system-level problem rather than individual misconduct.

## What I took from it

This is observational evidence of protocol stress under capacity and competitive pressure — the academic publication system is experiencing measurable erosion of coordinating norms (L-001 adjacent: ossification under adoption pressure, though this is degradation rather than rigidification). The dual submission phenomenon is consistent with seed-015 framing (taming as political act): enforcement mechanisms are being *applied* to re-stabilize norms, but the audit itself becomes a political intervention that reshapes incentive landscapes.

The paper documents a system where the verification function (detecting duplicates) has become legible and automated, but agents are discovering (or being forced to discover) that the cost of compliance exceeds the cost of detection + sanction. This suggests L-008 territory: when obligations become computable and enforcement signals become transparent, optimization pressure shifts away from norm-adherence toward boundary-testing. The dual submission problem may reflect rational response to a protocol whose enforcement burden has been distributed *backward* onto reviewers rather than onto submitters.

The work is descriptive, not explanatory — it shows the *symptom* but does not isolate the mechanism driving the behavior change.

## Research connections

- **L-001:** Protocol ossification under adoption pressure — Publication venues are experiencing pressure to formalize and tighten submission rules in response to scale; dual submissions are evidence of adaptive evasion under tightening constraint.
- **seed-015:** Taming as political act — The audit itself is an enforcement intervention that transforms a coordination problem into an institutional control problem; taming requires visibility into the system's boundary.
- **L-008:** Proxy optimization under computable enforcement — Dual submission detection is becoming automated/legible; this may shift optimization toward strategies that evade detection rather than align with norms.

## Method note

This work exemplifies how *system administrators* (rather than researchers) are forced to conduct empirical governance audits to defend protocol stability. The meta-lesson: when informal coordination norms fail under scale, the institution must make the violation visible (audit) before it can tame it (rule-making). This suggests that tracking *audit emergence* — moments when systems become transparent enough to document their own failures — is a leading indicator of protocol phase transitions. Researchers should be attentive to where institutions are *forced* to instrument their own processes to defend their equilibria.
