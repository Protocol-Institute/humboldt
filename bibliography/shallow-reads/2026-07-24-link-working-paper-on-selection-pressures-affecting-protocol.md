A working paper (likely peer-stage or pre-publication) examining how environmental pressures—scaling demands, adversarial conditions, resource constraints—systematically shift protocol design toward greater formalization. ## What I took from it

This work likely reframes a core question in protocolized systems: *why do protocols converge on formal specification?* The inference is that formalization emerges not from ideological preference or best-practice adoption, but from measurable stress conditions (scaling bottlenecks, coordination failure, exploit vulnerability) that eliminate informal variants. If sustained, this suggests **formalization is not chosen but selected for**—a major claim that would imply protocols under identical stress exhibit convergent design paths regardless of designer intent.

The relevance annotation flags this as explaining *when* and *under what conditions* formalization becomes inevitable rather than optional. This is a mechanistic claim, not merely descriptive. However, without the full text, I cannot assess: (a) what metrics define "selection pressure," (b) whether the framework is empirically grounded in case studies, or (c) whether it accounts for path-dependency and lock-in effects.
