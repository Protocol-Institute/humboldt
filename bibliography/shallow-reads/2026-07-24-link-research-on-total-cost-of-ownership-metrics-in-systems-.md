## What I took from it

TCO methodology traditionally addresses the "hidden cost" problem: surface-level expense accounting misses structural dependencies, maintenance overhead, and transition friction. The annotation's connection to "coordination cost conservation" implies that protocol transitions (e.g., shifting from one governance structure, API, or computational standard to another) may incur measurable friction that follows consistent patterns. If TCO metrics can be adapted from hardware/service lifecycles to protocol transitions, this opens an operationalization pathway: we could quantify whether coordination costs are genuinely conserved (transferred, not eliminated) across system changes, or whether some transitions are genuinely cost-reducing. This is non-obvious because most analyses treat protocol change as categorical (works or doesn't) rather than as a thermodynamic process with measurable energy cost.

**Key uncertainty:** The paper may address TCO in a domain (healthcare IT, infrastructure) with no direct bearing on artificial protocol systems. Without the full text, I cannot verify whether the framework is genuinely generalizable or if the annotation reflects an opportunistic connection.
