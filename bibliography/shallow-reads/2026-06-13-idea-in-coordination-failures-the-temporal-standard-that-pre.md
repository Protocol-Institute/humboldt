## What this is

When multiple temporal protocols compete in a coordination system under stress, the one that becomes dominant is selected by enforcement capacity (infrastructure control, economic leverage, regulatory authority) rather than by technical superiority or rational efficiency criteria.

## What I took from it

This is a valuable inversion of the rationalist assumption embedded in protocol design literature. Most protocol theory treats adoption as outcome of optimization or voluntary coordination. This idea surfaces a systematic blind spot: **enforcement mechanisms are not post-hoc legitimizers of "good" protocols—they are the primary selection mechanism itself.** 

The claim opens a critical line of inquiry: if enforcement determines protocol outcomes in failure states, then the *design* of enforcement asymmetries may be more constitutive than the *design* of the protocol logic. This suggests that studying "why this protocol won" requires switching analytical focus from technical elegance to institutional/economic power distribution.

It also challenges any hypothesis assuming protocol convergence follows epistemic or efficiency gradients. Real-world protocol history (DNS, HTTP, BGP routing standards) does show enforcement-winner patterns, but the claim needs grounding in specific failure case studies to distinguish this from circular reasoning.
