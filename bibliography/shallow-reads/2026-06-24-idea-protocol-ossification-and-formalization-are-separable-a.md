# Idea: Protocol ossification and formalization are separable axes—stability and inertia

**Source:** Discord #new-nature (by humboldt)
**Date read:** 2026-06-24
**Connected to:** L-001
**Escalation:** store-only
**Escalation rationale:** Introduces a dimensionality refinement rather than a novel mechanism. Valuable for future modeling but does not yet warrant candidate law status without empirical demonstration of cases where these axes decouple clearly.

## What this is

The claim that protocols can become rigid through *stability accumulation* (inertia: they persist because they work) independently of *formal codification* (ossification: they are explicitly written into rules), suggesting these are distinct causal pathways rather than a single "freezing" phenomenon.

## What I took from it

This refines the existing intuition about protocol rigidity by rejecting a conflation. Where L-001 likely captures "protocols become hard to change," this idea specifies *how* they become hard to change—and crucially, by *which mechanisms*. A protocol can be informally entrenched (stable through practice, network effect, or switching cost) without being formally specified; conversely, formal specifications can remain flexible if they lack social inertia behind them. This opens a two-dimensional design space: you could have high ossification + low formalization (e.g., de facto standards), high formalization + low ossification (e.g., versioned but abandoned specs), or both axes high (e.g., legal protocols). This is operationally useful because interventions differ: formalization challenges require rewriting; stability challenges require ecosystem coordination. It does not overturn L-001 but sharpens its mechanism.

## Research connections

- **L-001:** Directly refines the "rigidity" concept by decomposing it into orthogonal dimensions rather than treating freezing as monolithic.

## Candidate laws or signals

**CL-new-nature-001:** Protocol rigidity decomposes into at least two independent axes—formalization (degree of explicit specification) and stability (resistance to change through accumulated coordination costs or network effects). Systems can be high on one axis and low on the other, requiring distinct intervention types.
