## What this is

An econometric study of commercial satellite placement decisions within the ITU's administrative coordination regime for geostationary orbit. The paper models how operators locate along GEO longitude using spatial sorting methods, showing that placement correlates with income reachability below each longitude rather than with market price signals or property rights. The regime creates legible but non-tradeable position allocations.

## What I took from it

This is a live case of protocol ossification meeting strategic boundary concentration under formalized legibility. The ITU protocol—which records frequency assignments and orbital positions through national administrations without creating tradeable property or market price—appears to *prevent* efficient location discovery while simultaneously *enabling* strategic capture of position boundaries (the economically productive longitudes). Operators cannot freely trade positions, but the administrative legibility of who-holds-what creates incentives to concentrate in measurable, reachable income zones. This is L-014 in operation: computable legality (the ITU's formalized assignment system) drives strategic boundary concentration, because optimizing agents can see the payoff surface but cannot negotiate it through price. Simultaneously, the inability to *modify* the protocol (national administrations hold lock-in; spectrum is scarce and high-stakes) suggests L-001 friction: the protocol achieved critical adoption mass (global satellite operations depend on it) and is now resistant to restructuring even when it blocks value creation. The paper shows the protocol is *working* (satellites communicate, frequencies don't collide) while simultaneously being *stuck*—a clean instance of Gall's law (L-005) applied to infrastructure.

## Seed

**Seed title:** Formalization-Locked Allocation Under Non-Tradeable Legibility
**Seed type:** observation
**Seed text:** When a protocol formalizes and makes legible the allocation of a scarce resource (e.g., orbital position, spectrum) without creating tradeable property rights or market-clearing mechanisms, optimizing agents concentrate at the formalized boundaries that maximize reachable value. The regime exhibits simultaneous efficiency-loss and resistance-to-change: the protocol works well enough that replacement is dangerous (L-005), but the absence of price signals ensures that allocation remains suboptimal and strategic boundary concentration persists. This pattern should recur wherever administrative formalization precedes marketization, or where political/safety constraints prevent market mechanisms from operating on formally legible resources.
