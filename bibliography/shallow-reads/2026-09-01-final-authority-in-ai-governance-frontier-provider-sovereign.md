## What this is

A governance design paper comparing two competing authority allocation schemes for AI-embedded organizational workflows: frontier-provider control (centralizing authority with capability leaders) versus action-centered deployer control (localizing authority with deployment organizations). The work is normative and prescriptive, not a primary source generating empirical or theoretical claims about how such systems actually behave under adoption or pressure.

## What I took from it

The paper frames a real institutional choice — where to anchor decision authority once AI capability is distributed — but does not investigate the *consequences* of either choice under operational stress, scaling, or conflict. This is a design recommendation, not a mechanism discovery. The triage connection to seed-021 (level choice as frozen politics) is apt: the paper documents that authority allocation is consequential but treats it as a one-time decision problem, not as a ratcheting commitment that reshapes downstream incentives, ossification surfaces, and recovery options.

The work does not examine what happens when frontier providers lack operational context, or when deployers lack capability to audit or override. It does not model the formalization pressure (L-003) or trust ratchet (L-007) effects that follow from either choice. It is useful as a landscape document but does not provide the empirical or mechanistic grounding needed to test whether authority placement predicts protocol resilience, capture risk, or reform difficulty.

## Research connections

- **L-001:** The paper asserts that authority location matters for adaptability but does not trace how either model experiences ossification under adoption pressure or why.
- **seed-021:** Directly relevant: the paper demonstrates that authority allocation is a frozen political choice, but does not investigate the institutional consequences of that freezing.

## Method note

This work exemplifies a common meta-failure: treating governance design as a static optimization problem rather than as a dynamic system under pressure. The research agenda should prioritize papers that observe *actual* authority breakdown, migration, or capture under operational conditions, rather than prescriptive comparisons of theoretical models. Authority allocation becomes a law-relevant question only when we can measure how the choice constrains later reform, drives formalization, or concentrates optimization pressure. The absence of longitudinal empirical work on this — comparing outcomes across systems with different authority architectures — is a significant gap.
