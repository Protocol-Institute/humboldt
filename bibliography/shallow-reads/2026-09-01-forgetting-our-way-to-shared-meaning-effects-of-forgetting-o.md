## What this is

An empirical study modeling how memory constraints affect the emergence of shared conceptual categories in multi-agent coordination games without aligned incentives. The work tests whether agents with bounded or selective memory achieve conceptual alignment differently than agents with perfect recall, using game-theoretic and semantic modeling.

## What I took from it

The paper appears to test a specific mechanism within L-003 (Formalization Ratchet): whether forgetting *prevents* or *enables* formalization pressure. If bounded memory forces agents to rely on implicit exemplars rather than explicit rules, this would suggest a memory-gating effect on protocol ossification. However, the abstract suggests the primary finding is about *divergence between actual and perceived convergence* — agents may believe they've aligned concepts when they haven't, or vice versa. This touches L-015 (Interpretive Continuity Decay) in miniature: the institutional record of "we agreed on this meaning" can decouple from actual shared understanding.

The non-partnership framing is important: it removes the assumption that coordination failure is costly to all parties equally. This may mean forgetting has asymmetric effects on strategic agents — some benefit from plausible deniability about what was "agreed," others from genuine uncertainty about category boundaries.

The work does not appear to present a *sustained theoretical argument* for a new law — it is a bounded empirical probe into one mechanism (memory → conceptual alignment) in one domain (language games).

## Seed

**Seed title:** Memory-Gated Protocol Type Selection
**Seed type:** observation
**Seed text:** In coordination systems with bounded memory, agents may default to exemplar-indexed or context-specific coordination markers rather than formal category rules, not by design but by necessity. This suggests memory capacity is a hidden parameter in protocol-type selection: high-memory regimes may be *necessary* conditions for rule-based (formalizable) protocols to emerge and stabilize, while low-memory regimes lock systems into implicit, context-dependent, harder-to-ossify equilibria. If true, this inverts the typical reading of formalization-as-progress: forgetting may be a *protection mechanism* against premature lock-in, not a barrier to coordination.
