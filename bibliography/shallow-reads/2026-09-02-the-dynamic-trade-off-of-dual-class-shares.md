## What this is

Empirical economic study using 52 years of US dual-class firm data to measure the dynamic valuation and innovation effects of founder control structures. The paper documents that dual-class recapitalizations produce short-term valuation gains but long-term valuation decline, while innovation output persists — a temporal disaggregation of the classic agency cost trade-off.

## What I took from it

This is a careful measurement of a known phenomenon (founder control vs. diffuse ownership) rather than a theoretical or mechanistic contribution. The temporal dissociation — valuation rise then fall; innovation steady — is interesting as a descriptor but does not supply a new mechanism for why this pattern emerges. The work confirms that control concentration under firm-specific investment conditions resists dissolution (consistent with L-001 and L-005), but does not interrogate the *protocol dynamics* of why restructuring becomes progressively harder, nor does it examine the legibility, formalization, or coordination costs involved in the governance layer itself.

The paper treats dual-class structures as an economic choice variable, not as a protocol system subject to ossification pressure, formalization dynamics, or proxy capture. It measures outcomes but does not model the institutional stickiness or the mechanisms that convert short-term legitimacy into long-term lock-in.

## Research connections

- **L-001:** Confirms that control concentration (dual-class) persists and resists reversal, but does not explain the mechanism of ossification under adoption or stakeholder pressure.
- **L-005:** Consistent with the principle that working systems resist replacement, but does not examine the coordination or formalization costs that make restructuring unsafe.
- **seed-076 (Handler-Lodged Ossification in Opaque Protocols):** Tangentially — the founder's control function acts as a "handler" that becomes embedded; the paper shows persistence but not the legibility or opacity dynamics.
