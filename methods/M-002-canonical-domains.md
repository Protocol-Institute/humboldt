# M-002: Canonical Domains and Examples Set

**Type:** Generative + Analytical (dual-use)
**Purpose:** Maintain a set of deeply understood home domains that serve as analogy reservoirs, stress-test beds, and law-generation accelerators
**Maturity:** Active (first defined 2026-05-20)
**Current domain set:** `methods/canonical-domains.yaml`

---

## What This Technique Is For

Human researchers develop deep expertise in a small number of domains that they know well enough to reason about reliably without needing to look things up. These home domains serve multiple functions simultaneously:

- **Analogy reservoir:** when encountering an unfamiliar phenomenon, immediately ask whether it structurally resembles something in the canonical set
- **Stress-test bed:** when drafting a candidate law, immediately run it through the canonical domains to check scope — does it hold there? Does it break there?
- **Law-generation accelerator:** the domains are known deeply enough that new candidate laws can be generated by reasoning about domain structure, not just by retrieval
- **Calibration baseline:** the canonical domains contain well-understood empirical laws and formal theorems that serve as quality benchmarks — if a candidate law from an unknown domain resembles a proven result in cryptography or distributed systems, it gets higher initial confidence

The canonical set is small by design. Depth beats breadth. Knowing ten things about twenty domains is less useful than knowing a hundred things about five. The goal is to have domains where Humboldt can reason about second- and third-order consequences without needing to retrieve, because the mechanistic structure is internalized.

The set shifts as fertility changes. A domain that yields no new laws after sustained attention is deprioritized. A domain that repeatedly generates productive analogies gets deepened.

## Four Application Patterns

### Pattern A: Canonical Check (analytical)

When a candidate law is drafted, run it through the canonical set as the first stress-test:

1. State the law in domain-neutral terms
2. For each canonical domain, ask: does this law apply here? If yes, what does it predict? Is the prediction confirmed by known examples?
3. For each canonical domain where the law breaks: is the breakdown a counterexample (falsification) or a boundary condition (scope refinement)?
4. Update the law's domain list and confidence level accordingly

The canonical check is fast because the domains are familiar. It does not replace broader cross-domain triangulation (M-003) but precedes it — filter with the canonical set first, then triangulate broadly if the law survives.

### Pattern B: Canonical Pairing (generative — controlled M-001)

A more controlled variant of Random Links (M-001): instead of a fully random pairing, deliberately pair one canonical domain with one unfamiliar input. The canonical domain provides a reliable structural anchor; the unfamiliar input provides the novelty. This reduces the risk of the pairing being unproductive (you know the canonical domain well enough to find the connection if one exists) while preserving the cross-domain generative move.

Procedure: same as M-001, but step 3 (structural mechanism) is seeded from the canonical domain's known mechanistic structure rather than developed from scratch.

### Pattern C: Template Matching (analytical)

Each canonical domain contains instances of specific *types* of laws — mathematical constraints, impossibility theorems, statistical regularities, game-theoretic equilibria. When drafting a candidate law, identify which type it most resembles, then use the canonical domain's best instance of that type as a quality template.

Example: if a candidate law is a constraint theorem (at most two of three properties can coexist), CAP theorem is the template. The question becomes: can the candidate law be stated with CAP-like precision? What is the formal structure of the tradeoff?

The canonical law types and their exemplars:
| Law type | Canonical exemplar | Domain |
|----------|-------------------|--------|
| Mathematical necessity | RSA trapdoor function; elliptic curve hardness | Cryptography |
| Impossibility theorem | CAP theorem; FLP impossibility | Distributed systems |
| Statistical regularity | Zipf's law for city sizes; scaling laws | Urban environments |
| Systems dynamics | Bullwhip effect; bottleneck propagation | Supply chains |
| Game-theoretic equilibrium | Regulatory capture as Nash equilibrium | Political governance |

### Pattern D: Domain Rotation (generative)

Periodically rotate through the canonical domains and ask: what phenomena in this domain have I not yet examined for protocol laws? This is a scheduled generative move, not triggered by a specific question. Each rotation session picks one canonical domain and does a focused investigation: what are the least-examined corners of this domain? What phenomena here have no corresponding law in the inventory yet?

Rotation schedule: roughly monthly per domain, more frequent for domains currently yielding active hypotheses.

## Selection and Deselection Criteria

### What makes a domain canonical?

A domain earns canonical status by demonstrating:

1. **Formal richness:** the domain has documented laws, theorems, or empirical regularities that are well-understood and serve as reliable analogical sources
2. **Mechanistic clarity:** the causal mechanisms in the domain are understood well enough to reason about without retrieval — the domain has a "physics" that can be applied
3. **Protocol density:** the domain contains many distinct protocol-like structures (coordination mechanisms, enforcement regimes, formal specifications) not just one or two
4. **Cross-domain fertility:** the domain has already produced productive analogies to other domains in past work (either in the literature or in Humboldt's own research)
5. **Formal/informal spectrum:** the domain contains protocols at multiple levels of formalization, enabling comparison across the formalization ladder

### What triggers deselection?

- Three consecutive domain rotation sessions produce no new candidate laws or productive analogies
- The domain's known examples are consistently appearing in law files but never as the *generative* source — only as confirmatory evidence (this means the domain is an evidence source, not an analogy reservoir; it belongs in the bibliography, not the canonical set)
- A new domain consistently outperforms an existing one on fertility metrics

Deselected domains move to `bibliography/domains/` as reference material, not discarded. The canonical set is a rotating spotlight, not an exclusion list.

## The CAP Theorem as a Model for What Laws Should Look Like

The CAP theorem deserves special attention as a quality benchmark, not just as a domain example. It demonstrates what a mature protocol law looks like at its best:

- **Formally statable:** in any distributed system, at most two of {Consistency, Availability, Partition Tolerance} can be guaranteed simultaneously
- **Identifies a fundamental tradeoff:** not "X is hard" but "X, Y, and Z cannot coexist; you must choose"
- **Provably constraining:** derived from formal reasoning, not empirical generalization
- **Applies across a class:** holds for all distributed systems above a certain generality, not just specific implementations
- **Has known scope:** the theorem applies to specific formal conditions; refinements (PACELC, etc.) extend its scope or sharpen its conditions

The goal for Humboldt's law inventory is to find CAP-theorem equivalents in other protocol domains. Not necessarily with mathematical proof — but with comparable precision, tradeoff structure, and scope clarity. When a candidate law is being formalized, the question "can this be stated with CAP-like precision?" is a useful sharpening tool.

Candidate protocol-domain CAP equivalents to investigate:
- Is there a CAP-like tradeoff between protocol hardness, adaptability, and adoption speed? (Can a protocol be simultaneously maximally hard to circumvent, easily modifiable, and widely adoptable — or is this provably impossible?)
- Is there a constraint theorem for governance protocols relating legitimacy, efficiency, and scope?
- The bullwhip effect may contain an impossibility result: can information latency, inventory efficiency, and supply chain resilience be simultaneously optimized?

## Technique Refinement Notes

*2026-05-20 (initial):* The four application patterns distinguish two modes: generative (A produces candidates, B is controlled M-001, D is scheduled rotation) and analytical (C is template matching for quality control). In practice these will blur — a canonical check often generates a refinement that functions as a new candidate. The distinction is useful for scheduling (when to use which pattern) but should not be enforced rigidly.

The key risk with canonical domains is **over-fitting to home territory** — finding the same patterns repeatedly because you're always looking in the same places. The antidote is deliberate pairing of canonical domains with inputs from outside the set (Pattern B) and the periodic literature survey (Input Mechanism 1) which explicitly searches outside established grooves.
