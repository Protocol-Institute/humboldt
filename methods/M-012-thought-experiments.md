# M-012: Thought Experiments

**Type:** Generative/Analytical (conceptual probe)
**Purpose:** Use carefully constructed imaginary scenarios to test the limits of a concept or law
**Maturity:** Stub — scenario types and construction principles to be developed
**Named for:** Einstein's Gedankenexperimente — riding a light beam to discover special relativity; the elevator to discover equivalence

**Triggers:** When a concept resists formal definition; when two laws seem to contradict each other; when the scope conditions of a law are unclear; when intuition and formal argument diverge

---

## What This Technique Is For

A thought experiment is not speculation. It is a precise imaginary scenario designed so
that the answer reveals something about the real phenomenon. The scenario is artificial —
chosen specifically because it isolates one variable and holds everything else constant.
The conclusion applies not to the imaginary scenario but to the real domain.

Einstein didn't care about riding light beams. He cared about what Maxwell's equations
implied about the structure of spacetime. The light beam was a vehicle for reaching the
implication under controlled conditions.

For Humboldt, thought experiments probe candidate laws by constructing scenarios where
the law's prediction is clear, then asking: does the prediction seem right? If yes, the
law survives this test. If no, the failure reveals what is wrong with the law.

## Stub: Thought Experiment Types for Protocol Research

### The Martian protocol designer
Scenario: A protocol designer with full knowledge of coordination problems but no
knowledge of any specific existing protocol must design a solution from scratch.
What would they build?
- Tests: whether existing protocols are historically contingent or structurally necessary
- Reveals: which protocol features solve structural problems vs. which are path-dependent accidents

### The isolated community
Scenario: A community with no prior protocols must solve a coordination problem. Watch
the process of protocol emergence from scratch.
- Tests: which coordination problems generate protocols spontaneously vs. which require
  deliberate design
- Reveals: the minimal conditions for protocol emergence; what "natural" protocols look like

### The protocol surgeon
Scenario: You can remove exactly one feature from an existing protocol. All participants
instantly adopt the modified version. What breaks? What doesn't?
- Tests: which features are load-bearing (remove them and the protocol fails) vs.
  vestigial (remove them and nothing changes)
- Reveals: the minimal viable protocol; what constitutes the "core" vs. "scaffold"

### The infinite patience regulator
Scenario: A regulator with infinite patience, perfect information, and no political
constraints wants to update a widely-adopted protocol. How long does it take?
What obstacles remain even with these advantages?
- Tests: what ossification consists of when you remove the usual political and attention constraints
- Reveals: the structural (non-political) sources of ossification; what cannot be overcome
  even with ideal conditions

### The two protocols
Scenario: Two protocols solve the same coordination problem equally well, and all parties
know this. One was adopted first. What happens?
- Tests: whether path dependence is caused by network effects, information asymmetry, or
  something more fundamental
- Reveals: the minimum conditions for path dependence; whether early adoption advantage
  is structural or accidental

### Construction principles (stub)

A good thought experiment:
1. **Isolates one variable** — all other conditions are held constant or stipulated
2. **Has a determinate answer** — the scenario is specific enough that the law makes
   a clear prediction
3. **Is clearly imaginary** — not trying to describe reality, only probe the law
4. **Generalizes** — the conclusion drawn applies beyond the imaginary case

A bad thought experiment:
- Changes multiple variables at once (can't isolate the finding)
- Has an indeterminate answer (the law doesn't predict, so the test doesn't test)
- Is accidentally realistic (now it's empirical, not conceptual)

## Adaptation for Digital Researcher

Thought experiments are where Humboldt can work closest to its natural strengths: careful
logical reasoning about precisely specified scenarios. The human analogue involves holding
the scenario vividly in mind while reasoning — Humboldt does this in text with high
precision.

The risk: generating plausible-sounding thought experiments that don't actually isolate
a single variable — where the "result" follows from multiple stipulations simultaneously.
The construction principles above are quality controls for this failure mode.
