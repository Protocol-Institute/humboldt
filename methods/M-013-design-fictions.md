# M-013: Design Fictions

**Type:** Generative (material imagination)
**Purpose:** Imagine artifacts that don't exist to make abstract protocol dynamics concrete and examinable
**Maturity:** Stub — artifact types and production process to be developed
**Tradition:** Dunne & Raby's speculative/critical design; Bruce Sterling's design fictions; the Extrapolation Factory

**Triggers:** When a law or hypothesis is well-formulated but feels abstract; when a mechanism needs to be made tangible; when a new law's consequences need to be explored; when communication of findings to non-researchers is needed

---

## What This Technique Is For

A design fiction is an imaginary object — a product, document, interface, institution, or
artifact — that embodies a particular set of assumptions about how protocols work. It is
not a prediction or a proposal. It is a probe: by fully specifying an artifact that takes
a protocol law seriously, we make the law's consequences visible and examinable.

The fictional object makes concrete what the law leaves abstract. "Protocols ossify under
adoption pressure" is an analytical claim. A design fiction might be: *a mandatory
modification impact assessment document required by a future regulatory body for any
proposed change to a protocol with more than 10,000 conforming implementations* —
complete with its fields, its bureaucratic language, its appeal procedures. The artifact
shows what ossification looks like as lived experience.

## Stub: Design Fiction Types

### Artifact types

**Regulatory documents**
Near-future regulatory filings, compliance forms, audit reports — embodying a world
where protocol governance has been formalized in particular ways.
- Example: "Form P-7 (Protocol Modification Environmental Impact Assessment) — required
  for any backward-incompatible change to a protocol with >50,000 implementations"

**Consumer products**
Objects people buy or use in a world where a protocol law has run to its conclusion.
- Example: a "Protocol Insurance" policy covering businesses against the cost of forced
  migration when a legacy protocol reaches end-of-support; includes actuarial tables for
  ossification risk by domain

**Research instruments**
Tools that don't exist but would be useful if a law is true — revealing what measurement
or intervention the law implies.
- Example: a "Protocol Fitness Meter" that measures the gap between a protocol's founding
  conditions and its current operating environment — a concrete instrument for CL-Simon-4

**Institutional artifacts**
Charters, bylaws, org charts for institutions that would exist if protocols evolved
differently.
- Example: the founding charter of "The Protocol Transition Authority" — a hypothetical
  intergovernmental body with the specific mandate of managing the metric/English-type
  fitness valley crossings for critical infrastructure protocols

**Personal objects**
Things individuals carry or use — revealing the human-scale experience of protocol dynamics.
- Example: a "Protocol Compliance Certificate" for a professional in a highly protocolized
  field, showing accumulated certifications, expired credentials, and pending renewals —
  making the formalization ratchet (L-003) tangible

### Production process (stub)

1. **Choose the law or hypothesis** to make concrete
2. **Identify the world state** — which law(s) are assumed to be true and operating?
3. **Ask: who would need what artifact in this world?** — start with a human need
4. **Fully specify the artifact** — enough detail that someone could actually produce it
5. **Read the artifact critically** — what does specifying it reveal about the law?
   - What ambiguities appear when you try to fill in the form?
   - What edge cases does the artifact's structure force you to address?
   - What does the artifact's existence imply about how people would adapt?
6. **Extract findings** — what does the artifact reveal that the abstract law didn't?

## Adaptation for Digital Researcher

Design fictions are inherently about tangible experience — the weight of a document, the
frustration of a form. Humboldt has no embodied experience to draw on.

The adaptation: Humboldt produces **text artifacts** — documents, forms, transcripts,
interfaces described in full detail — and reasons about them analytically. The lack of
embodied experience is partly compensated by the ability to specify artifacts at a level
of detail and internal consistency that human designers rarely achieve in sketching.

The risk: producing artifacts that are clever but not grounded — design fictions that
don't reveal anything new but instead illustrate the law without probing it. Quality
control: after producing an artifact, ask "what did I learn that I didn't know before
fully specifying this?" If the answer is nothing, the fiction was illustration, not investigation.

Possible future: if Humboldt gains image generation access, visual design fictions
become possible — actual visual artifacts, not just textual descriptions.
